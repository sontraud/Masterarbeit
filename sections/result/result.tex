\chapter{Simulation Results}
\label{ch:results}
in this chapter we investigate the impact of the parameters used in the \MP method on the simulation results.
We begin by looking at each parameter isolated to study their individual effects on the bed height.
Subsequently, we examine the impact of both damping terms and explain why the collision damping term is switched off during the parameter study.
We analyze the interaction of the parameters by varying two coefficients at a time and comparing the results to data provided by the "Division of Combustion Technology" of the "Engler-Bunte-Institute" \footnote{\url{https://vbt.ebi.kit.edu/english/21_1186.php}}.
%Following the parameter study we identify an optimal parameter set for our case and look at the simulation.
Finally we conclude by discussing limitations of the \MP method.



\section{Single Parameter Testing}
\label{sec:oneParam}
We start by recalling the explicit Harris and Crighton model
\begin{equation}
    \scs = \dfrac{P_s \pvf^\beta}{\max [( \pvfcp - \pvf), \varepsilon ( 1- \pvf)]}
\end{equation}
with the close pack volume fraction $\pvfcp$, the exponent $\beta$ and the pressure constant $P_s$.
 %$165251$


% \subsection{Influence of the Exponent β} In the reference Goldschmidt case, the stress follows a quadratic dependency (β=2). To analyze the influence of β, we express the particle volume fraction as a fraction of the packing limit: \begin{equation} \alpha_p = \lambda \alpha_{cp} \quad \text{where} \quad \lambda \in [0,1). \end{equation} Substituting this into a simplified form of the solid stress equation yields: \begin{equation} \tau_s \propto \frac{P_s (\lambda \alpha_{cp})^\beta}{\alpha_{cp} - \lambda \alpha_{cp}} = P_s \alpha_{cp}^{\beta -1} \frac{\lambda^\beta}{1-\lambda}. \end{equation} The term λβ dominates the behavior for lower volume fractions. Since λ<1, a smaller exponent β results in a larger value for λβ. Consequently, for lower β, the solid stress τs​ becomes significant at lower volume fractions, resulting in a more expanded bed. As β increases, the "activation" of the stress is delayed until the particles are more densely packed, causing the mean bed height to decrease exponentially, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:meanBedBeta}.

\subsection{Constant $\beta$}
In the reference Goldschmidt case the stress has a quadratic dependency on the particle volume fraction, therefor $\beta = 2$.
To analyze the effect of varying $\beta$ on our simulation, we rewrite the particle volume fraction as
\begin{align*}
    \pvf = \lambda \pvfcp \qquad \lambda \in [0,1),
\end{align*}
and insert it into a simplified version of the particle solid stress
\begin{align}
    \scs = \dfrac{P_s \pvf^\beta}{\pvfcp - \pvf} = P_s\dfrac{\lambda^\beta}{(1-\lambda)} \pvfcp^{\beta -1}
\end{align}
thus yielding 
\begin{align*}
    \nabla_{\lambda } \scs = P_s \left(\dfrac{\beta \lambda^{\beta -1}}{1 - \lambda}  + \dfrac{\lambda^\beta}{(1- \lambda)^2}\right) \pvfcp^{\beta - 1}.
\end{align*}
We can see from both terms that the solid particle stress diverges for the volume fraction approaching close pack. 
For smaller $\lambda$ the term $\pvfcp^{\beta -1}$ dominates the gradient.
For $\lambda$ approaching one, therefor a close packed bed, the first term starts to dominate. 
% For a smaller $\beta$ this occurs earlier leading to a higher particle normal stress gradient for smaller particle volume fractions.
% Decreasing $\beta$ 
% For smaller $\beta$, the leading factor rises faster, thus $\scs$ is higher for lower volume fractions. %In the plot for $\beta = 1$, we can observe this behavior in both the implicit \ref{fig:betaImp1} and the explicit \ref{fig:betaExp1} case, where the particles' position exceeds the plotted area.
Due to the higher particle acceleration for lower volume fraction the bed expands for smaller $\beta$.
Plotting the bed height for increasing values of $\beta$ reveals an exponential decay, see fig \ref{fig:meanBedBeta}.
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering

For a higher bed height the particles are further spread apart leading to a lower pressure at the bottom of the grid. For higher exponents the bed height decrease leading to a more densely packed bed., resulting in an increase in pressure drop before declining again.

\subsection{Solid Pressure Constant}
The solid pressure constant $P_s$ linearly scales the particle normal stress gradient.
A higher solid pressure constant results in particles being accelerated even for low particle fractions, leading to a linear increase in bed height, see figure \ref{fig:meanBedPS}. An increase in bed height again leads to a decrease in pressure drop.

\subsection{Volume Fraction Close Packed}
The gradient of the particle normal stress diverges as the particle volume fraction approaches close packed. If the close packed volume fraction is set to a lower value, the singularity is reached earlier, causing particles to be accelerated at lower concentrations. This leads to an increased bed height, see fig. \ref{fig:meanBedPacked} and an increase in pressure drop.
From experiments \cite{closePack}, we know that random sphere packing generally has a particle volume fraction of $0.63$. An upper limit is given by the Kepler conjecture, which states that sphere packing cannot exceed $ \sim 0.74$. 
In the \MP method particles are modeled as point masses rather than resolved spheres and therefor geometric constraints are not enforced.
Consequently, we can increase the close packed volume fraction further than theoretically possible.

\subsection{Results Return-to-Isotropy}
\label{sec:returnToIsotropyImpact}

The second damping term introduces a stochastic element, by modifying the particle velocities based on a velocity distribution to imitate the scattering from collisions, see sec. \ref{sec:isotropy} and sec. \ref{sec:MPLoop}.
In \OF the unmodified \MP solver is run with the fixed seed \lstinline|123456|, therefore the same random variables are sampled and the collision term is applied to the same particles each time the simulation is run ensuring reproducibility.
We run the simulation fifty times with identical set up and a random seed.
Data is sampled from the same $1000$ probe locations as before and compared over all $50$ simulations.
We calculate a mean value of each probe for each time step and calculate the maximal deviation from the mean particle volume fraction as well as the mean deviation, see figure \ref{fig:maxDiffIso}.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\linewidth]{sections/result/max_abw.eps}
    %\includegraphics[width = 0.49 \linewidth]{sections/result/mean_abw.eps}
    \includegraphics[width = 0.49 \linewidth]{sections/result/mean_mean_abw.eps}
    \caption{Maximal difference of fluid volume to the mean fluid volume fraction (left) and mean deviation of all cells averaged in each time step (right) in $1000$ points.}
    \label{fig:maxDiffIso}
\end{figure}

After the particles are inserted we see a spike in the mean deviation.
After the particles have settled into a bed the particles are accelerated by the fluid inlet velocity a second spike appears in the mean deviation and the maximal deviation at $t = 1.1s$.
The spike in maximal deviation is due to a layer of fluid forming in the particle bed in some of the cases, as depicted in figure \ref{fig:diffIsoPV}.
This leads to a large local difference in the fluid volume fraction.

Although the mean and maximal deviation stabilizes, longer simulation times could amplify the effects of the return-to-isotropy model.

Fitting parameters is done by minimizing the relative error for the data 
 \begin{equation}
 \label{eq:relError}
        err = \sqrt{\left( \dfrac{\Delta p - \Delta p_{exp}}{\Delta p_{exp}}\right)^2 + \left( \dfrac{h_{bed} - h_{bed,exp}}{h_{bed,exp}} \right)^2}.
\end{equation}


In the following we go over all three cases for the velocity of $0.015 \operatorname{m/s}$ and look at the relative error of the pressure, the bed height and the combined relative error defined in equation \eqref{eq:relError}.
The results are discussed in sec \ref{sec:case1}, \ref{sec:case2}, \ref{sec:case3}
and summarized in sec. \ref{sec:discAndRes}.

While the \MP method shows promising results for modeling fluidized beds it is not adequate for modeling a fixed bed.
When initializing particles inside the reactor with negligible fluid inlet velocity, we would expect them to settle into a static bed, with particle velocities close to zero.
Simulating this case wit \lstinline|MPPICFoam| and a gas inlet velocity of $10^{-6} m/s$, the particles don not reach said state.
The average particle velocity after five seconds is about $0.2 m/s$.
This is due to the particle stress model, which accelerates particles near or in close packed regions, as in our densely packed bed.
To illustrate this we track one of the particles over the first two seconds in figure \ref{fig:part_moves_packed}. %Particle ID 144135
