\section{Iron Reduction}
Iron is used in a reduction-oxidation cycle. Reducing iron oxide can be done via hydrogen for which several industrial-scale technologies exist such as the steam-iron redox process. 
For this cycle iron oxide is reduced
\begin{align*}
    \ce{Fe2O3 + 3H2 -> 2Fe + 3H2O}
\end{align*}
which is an endothermic process. The stored iron is then oxidized to iron oxide which is a exothermic process. 
As the efficiency of the iron storage cycle depends on the efficiency of both the oxidation and the reduction step both need to be as efficient as possible.
The goal of this master thesis is a numerical simulation of a simplified reduction in a fluidized bed reactor.



\section{Fluidized Bed Reactor}
A fluidized bed reactor suspends solid particles in an upward-flowing fluid, typically a gas, so that the particles behave like a fluid.

In figure \ref{fig:fluidize_states} the different fluidization regimes in a fluidized bed reactor are illustrated.
On the left, the gas inflow rate is low. The gas percolates through the empty spaces in-between the particles. The particles form a fixed bed and particles are still stationary.
As the flow rate increases, the bed begins to expand.
In the second illustration the inflow velocity is increased until minimum fluidization $\u_{mf}$, where the upward drag force equals the gravitational force on the particles.
The particles are suspended by the upward flow of the gas.
% Beyond this point, the particles are suspended by the upward flow of the gas and the bed exhibits fluid-like behavior.
A dense-phase fluidized bed, a bed in which the surface of the bed is still fairly observable, behaves like a fluid.
Fluid-like properties, such as a light object floating to the top of the reactor and the surface staying horizontal in a tilted reactor.

Depending on the inflowing gas velocity different and properties of the solid particles the behavior of the bed varies.
At higher flow rates the bed does not expand much beyond its minimum fluidization volume. Large instabilities and bubbles start to form leading to a bubbling fluidized bed.
Bubbles form at the bottom of the bed and coalesce into larger bubbles.
When further increasing the gas velocity an upper surface of the bed can no longer be observed.
The bed extrudes a turbulent motion and a turbulent fluidized bed is formed.
Even further is the pneumatic transport of solids in which the solids are carried out of the bed by the gas.

To determine the fluidization behavior several different particles properties and their influence have been studied in the past.
The first is the velocity minimum fluidization velocity $\umf$.
At the minimum fluidization velocity the drag force exhibited by the upward moving gas is at equilibrium with the weight of the particles.
The drag force can be expressed as the pressure drop across the bed times the cross section of the reactor
\begin{equation*}
    F_D = \Delta \p A_r.
\end{equation*}
For the weight of the particles we use the volume fraction, the volume of the bed and the specific weight of solids $\gamma = \rho \g$
\begin{equation*}
    F_{m_{mf}} = A_r L_{mf} \pvf_{mf} ((\rp - \rf) \g).
\end{equation*}
%smaller particles -> higher pressure drop (trade off)
Equating both forces, we can calculate a ratio of pressure drop to the bed height at minimum fluidization height
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:prDrop}
    \dfrac{\Delta \p}{L_{mf}} = \pvf \Delta \rho \g.
\end{equation}
Assuming that the pressure of the particles dominates the pressure difference, $\rp \gg \rf$ we can estimate the pressure drop at minimum fluidization velocity by
\begin{equation*}
    \Delta \p \approx \pvf L_{mf} \rp g = \dfrac{\m \g}{A_r}.
\end{equation*}
Therefor, above minimum fluidization velocity the pressure drop stays constant. 
For uniformly sized particles during velocities inducing a fixed bed, the pressure drop is nearly proportional.
During the transition from a fixed bed to a fluidized be the maximal pressure is reached due to adhesive forces. Similarly to a liquid the pressure drop then remains constant during the fluidized bed phase.

Investigation of flow through fixed beds has led to several empirical models to predict the pressure drop. 
The frictional pressure drop was empirically correlated to the cube Reynolds number
\begin{equation*}
    F_D = (A \pvf + B \operatorname{Re}_p) \dfrac{\pvf \mu_f}{\fvf^2 d_p^2} \qquad \operatorname{Re}_p = \dfrac{\rf \lvert \u_{rel} \rvert d_p}{\mu_f}
\end{equation*}
Several different coefficient values have been proposed for $A$ and $B$. %\Todo{Ein paar schreiben}
Two models used in \OF are the Plessis-Masliyah drag model \cite{Plessis}
\begin{equation*}
    A = \dfrac{26.8 \fvf^2}{\pvf^{2/3}(1-\pvf^{1/3})(1-\fvf^{2/3}} \qquad B = \dfrac{\fvf^2}{(1-\pvf^{2/3})^2}
\end{equation*}
and the Ergun-Wen-Yu model \cite{ErgunWenYu}
\begin{equation*}
    F_D = \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{3 (1-\fvf) \mu_f \fvf \operatorname{Re}_p}{d_p^2} C_D \fvf^{-2.65} & \text{if } \fvf \geq 0.8 \\
        \left(150 \dfrac{1-\fvf}{\fvf} + 1.75 \operatorname{Re}_p \right) \dfrac{(1-\fvf) \mu_f}{d_p^2} & \text{if } \fvf < 0.8
    \end{cases} \qquad C_D = \begin{cases}
        24 \left(1+0.15 \operatorname{Re}_p^{0.687}\right) & \text{if } \operatorname{Re}_p < 1000 \\
        0.44 & \text{if } \operatorname{Re}_p \geq 1000
    \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
which uses the Ergun expression for fluid volume fractions below $0.8$.

Two empirically calculated velocities are shown. The first is the minimal fluidization velocity discussed earlier.
It is based on the empirical correlation by Ergun for the pressure drop
\begin{equation*}
    \dfrac{\Delta \p}{L} = 150 \dfrac{\nu (1-\fvf_{mf})^2}{\fvf_{mf}^3 d_{32}^2} \umf + 1.75 \dfrac{(1-\fvf_{mf}) \rf}{\fvf_{mf}^3 d_{32}} \umf^2
\end{equation*}
which we then combine with equation \eqref{eq:prDrop}
\begin{equation*}
    \dfrac{1.75}{\fvf_{mf}^3 \phi_s} \operatorname{Re}^2_{p,mf} + \dfrac{150 (1-\fvf_{mf})}{\fvf_{mf}^3 \phi_s^2} \operatorname{Re}_{p,mf} = \operatorname{Ar} \qquad \operatorname{Ar} = \dfrac{d_p^3 \rf (\rp - \rf) \g}{\mu ^2}.
\end{equation*}
which we rewrite as
\begin{equation*}
    \operatorname{Ar} = K_1 \operatorname{Re}_{p,mf} + K_2 \operatorname{Re}^2_{p,mf}.
\end{equation*}

Another velocity shown in the regime diagram, that greatly impacts the behavior of particles is the terminal free-fall velocity. This represents a equilibrium of the gravitational force, 
Keeping the velocity above $\umf$ and below $\u_t$ reduces carry over of particles from a fluidized bed.
To calculate $\u_t$ we use the diameter of the smallest particles 
\begin{equation*}
    \u_t = \left[ \dfrac{4 d_{p,s} (\rp - \rf)\g}{3 \rf C_D}\right]^{1/2}.
\end{equation*}

\section{Numerical Simulation}
To simulate a fluidized bed reactor we can look at how multiphase systems are generally modeled in CFD. 
We can classify CFD simulations by how the solid phase is modeled, whether we model it as a continuum or a discrete phase, while the gas phase is always modeled as a continuum. 
The models differ in how particle-particle interactions are handled.
Using a solver with a discrete particle phase, such as the \DP method in \OF we can model each particle-particle interaction, therefor each collision and the resulting behavior of the particles. With an increasing number of particles, the simulation time grows quadratically with the number of particles, which is often not feasible for industrial scale applications.
Here the trend of the experiment is often more important than the exact behavior.
For these cases one could consider modeling the particle as a continuum would result in an Euler-Euler approach where particle-particle interactions needed for closure relations.
In general this leads to a computationally less expensive method that does not account for particle-particle interactions \cite{mathMPPIC}, especially in dense systems.
To mitigate the disadvantages from the previously mentioned methods we will use a hybrid approach which models particle interactions through the particle normal stress and damping terms without resolving them, called the \MP method.

In this method the particles are modeled as both a discrete and a continuous phase with interpolation operators being used to transfer properties between the two.
The main advantage of this method is, that particle-particle interactions are calculated from parameters that only depend on the Eulerian grid.
