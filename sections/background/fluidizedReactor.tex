\section{Iron Reduction}
In this master thesis we look at iron as a
carbon-free energy carrier in a reduction-oxidation cycle. Reducing iron oxide can be done via hydrogen for which several industrial-scale technologies exist such as the steam-iron redox process \cite{coalToIron}. 
For this cycle iron oxide is reduced
\begin{align*}
    \ce{Fe2O3 + 3H2 -> 2Fe + 3H2O}
\end{align*}
which is an endothermic process. The stored iron is then oxidized to iron oxide which is a exothermic process. 
As the efficiency of the iron storage cycle depends on the efficiency of both the oxidation and the reduction step both need to be as efficient as possible.
The goal of this master thesis is a numerical simulation of a simplified reduction in a fluidized bed reactor.

\section{Fluidized Bed Reactor}
\label{sec:basicFBR}

Recent experiments have shown the possibility of reducing iron oxide via a fluidized bed reactor \cite{Malte}.
Particles are either inserted into the reactor via a chute or placed inside the reactor on top of a porous plate. The accumulated particles are called a bed of particles. 
The particles are suspended by an upward-flowing fluid, in our case gas, causing the particles to behave like a fluid. The behavior and properties are described in \cite{zustand_eng}.
In figure \ref{fig:fluidize_states} the different fluidization regimes in a fluidized bed reactor are illustrated.
On the left, the gas inflow rate is low. The gas percolates through the empty spaces in-between the particles. The particles form a fixed bed and particles are still stationary.
As the flow rate increases, the bed begins to expand.
In the second illustration the inflow velocity is increased until minimum fluidization $\u_{mf}$, where the upward drag force equals the gravitational force on the particles.
The particles are suspended by the upward flow of the gas.
% Beyond this point, the particles are suspended by the upward flow of the gas and the bed exhibits fluid-like behavior.
A dense-phase fluidized bed, a bed in which the surface of the bed is still fairly observable, behaves like a fluid.
Fluid-like properties, such as a light object floating to the top of the reactor and the surface staying horizontal in a tilted reactor.

Depending on the inflowing gas velocity different and properties of the solid particles the behavior of the bed varies.
At higher flow rates the bed does not expand much beyond its minimum fluidization volume. Large instabilities and bubbles start to form leading to a bubbling fluidized bed.
Bubbles form at the bottom of the bed and coalesce into larger bubbles.
When further increasing the gas velocity an upper surface of the bed can no longer be observed.
The bed extrudes a turbulent motion and a turbulent fluidized bed is formed.
Even further is the pneumatic transport of solids in which the solids are carried out of the bed by the gas.

The diagram in figure \ref{fig:fluidizeDiagram} relates the dimensionless particle diameter 
\begin{equation*}
 d_p^* = d_p \sqrt[3]{\dfrac{\rf \g \Delta \rho}{\eta_c^2}}.
\end{equation*}
and the dimensionless gas velocities
\begin{equation*}
u^* = \uf \sqrt[3]{\dfrac{\rf^2}{\eta_c \Delta\rho \g}}    
\end{equation*}
and classifies different fluidization regimes. Here $\rf$ is the gas density, $g$ is the gravitational force, $\Delta \rho$ is the difference between the solid and gas density and $\eta_c$ is the kinematic gas viscosity.

In addition to the minimum fluidization velocity the diagram also shows the terminal free fall velocity. 
This represents an equilibrium between gravitational and drag forces acting on a particle.
Keeping the velocity between the minimum fluidization and terminal free fall velocity is recommended to reduce carry over of particles from a fluidized bed.

\section{Numerical Simulation}
Before we simulate a fluidized bed reactor we first look at how multiphase systems are generally modeled in CFD \cite{cfdComp}.
We can classify CFD simulations by how the solid phase is modeled, whether we model it as a continuum or a discrete phase, while the gas phase is always modeled as a continuum. 
The models differ in how particle-particle interactions are handled.
Using a solver with a discrete particle phase, such as the \DP method in \OF we can model each particle-particle interaction, therefor each collision and the resulting behavior of the particles. With an increasing number of particles, the simulation time grows quadratically with the number of particles, which is often not feasible for industrial scale applications, where the trend of the experiment is often more important than the exact behavior.
For these cases one could consider modeling the particle as a continuum using an Euler-Euler approach where particle-particle interactions are needed for closure relations.
In general this leads to a computationally less expensive method that does not account for particle-particle interactions, leading to inaccurate results, especially in dense systems \cite{mathMPPIC}.
To mitigate the disadvantages from the previously mentioned methods we will use a hybrid approach which models particle as a discrete phase but models interactions through the particle normal stress and damping terms without resolving them, called the \MP method.

In this method the particles are modeled as both a discrete and a continuous phase with interpolation operators being used to transfer properties between the two.
The main advantage of this method is, that particle-particle interactions are calculated from parameters that only depend on the Eulerian grid and are not fully resolved.
We will discuss in detail how the \MP method works by first looking at the paper that presented the method in chapter \ref{ch:MPPIC} and then at the \OF implementation discussed in chapter \ref{ch:OpenFOAM}.
